Anxiety · panic · low mood · depression

Tools for hard moments, and a map of what helps

Quick calming exercises you can use right now, the core diagrams from cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), and a plain summary of the evidence on therapies, medicines, supplements and newer brain-based treatments, drawn from NICE, the NHS, Mayo Clinic, NIMH and recent trials.

Please read: this site gives general information, not medical advice, and it does not replace a GP, psychologist or psychiatrist. Do not start, stop or combine medicines or supplements without checking with a doctor or pharmacist. If you are thinking about ending your life or are unsafe, use the numbers in the red bar above now.

Calm now

Breathing timer

Pick a pattern, press Start and follow the shape. Breathe gently; you should not feel dizzy. If you do, stop and breathe normally.

Cyclic sigh

Promising One randomised trial

Two inhales through the nose (a full breath, then a short top-up), then a long, slow exhale through the mouth. In a Stanford trial of 111 healthy adults doing 5 minutes a day for a month, this exhale-focused pattern improved mood and lowered resting breathing rate more than mindfulness meditation. It has not yet been tested in people with diagnosed anxiety disorders.

Box breathing

Promising Same trial, widely used

In 4, hold 4, out 4, hold 4. The even rhythm gives the mind something to count, which helps when thoughts are racing. It was one of the arms in the same Stanford trial and also improved mood, slightly less than cyclic sighing.

Slow breathing 4-6

Good evidence Meta-analysis

In 4, out 6, which is about six breaths a minute. This is close to the "resonance" rate used in heart-rate-variability biofeedback, which a meta-analysis found reduces self-reported stress and anxiety. A longer out-breath leans on the calming (parasympathetic) side of the nervous system.

Panic first aid

What is happening: a surge of adrenaline is preparing your body to run or fight. The racing heart, tight chest, tingling and dizziness are unpleasant but are the body's alarm system, not a sign of danger. The NHS notes that most panic attacks last between 5 and 20 minutes.

What helps: stay where you are if it is safe, slow the out-breath, name it ("this is panic, it will pass"), and let the wave rise and fall rather than fighting it.

What keeps panic going: leaving the situation, sitting down "in case I faint", carrying water or pills "just in case". These safety behaviours bring short-term relief but teach the brain that the danger was real. CBT for panic gradually drops them.

See a doctor if chest pain, breathlessness or fainting are new, or if you are not sure it is panic. Call 999 if you think it could be a heart attack.

Two-minute muscle release

Good evidence NICE lists applied relaxation as a treatment for generalised anxiety

  1. Clench both fists for 5 seconds, then let go for 10. Notice the difference.
  2. Shrug shoulders to ears, hold 5, drop and soften.
  3. Scrunch face, hold 5, release your jaw and forehead.
  4. Press feet into the floor, tense legs, hold 5, release.
  5. Finish with three slow out-breaths.

Full applied relaxation is taught over several weeks by a therapist; this is a short version.

Worry time

Set a 15-minute slot each day. When a worry turns up outside it, jot it down and tell yourself "later". At worry time, use the worry tree below. Many people find most worries have faded by then.

The next small step

When mood is low, motivation usually follows action rather than coming first. Pick one task that takes under five minutes: open the curtains, shower, step outside, message one person. Do it, then notice how you feel. This is the core of behavioural activation.

Understand the cycle

CBT diagrams

CBT is based on the idea that thoughts, feelings, body sensations and actions feed each other. You cannot always change the situation, but you can step into the loop at the thought or the behaviour.

What the guidelines say

Treatment steps

UK care follows a "stepped" model: start with the least intensive treatment likely to work, and step up if it doesn't. Your preference matters. NICE's 2022 depression guideline tells clinicians to discuss options and support people's choice.

Depression (NICE NG222)

1

Less severe depression

Guided self-help is the first option for most people, followed by group CBT, group behavioural activation, individual CBT or behavioural activation, group exercise, mindfulness or meditation groups, interpersonal therapy, counselling or short psychodynamic therapy. Antidepressants are not offered routinely as first-line unless that is your preference.
2

More severe depression

A combination of individual CBT and an antidepressant is the most effective first option. Behavioural activation, psychotherapy or an antidepressant alone are alternatives.
3

Not improving

Review the diagnosis and adherence, change or combine treatments, or refer to specialist mental health services.
4

Treatment-resistant

Specialist options: medicine augmentation (for example lithium or an antipsychotic), ECT, rTMS, and in some countries esketamine. See newer treatments.

NICE uses a PHQ-9 score of 16 as a rough line between "less severe" and "more severe".

Generalised anxiety and panic (NICE CG113)

1

Recognise and explain

Information about anxiety and active monitoring.
2

Low-intensity help

Guided or self-guided CBT self-help (books or online) and psychoeducational groups.
3

High-intensity therapy or medicine

Individual CBT or applied relaxation, or an SSRI. Sertraline is suggested first for generalised anxiety. For panic disorder, CBT is the main treatment.
4

Complex or severe

Specialist assessment and combined treatment.

NICE advises against benzodiazepines for panic disorder and, for generalised anxiety, only as a short-term measure in a crisis.

Foundations

Daily habits with real evidence

Exercise

Strong BMJ 2024, 218 trials

A network meta-analysis of 218 trials and over 14,000 people found walking or jogging, yoga and strength training were the most effective forms of exercise for depression, with bigger effects at higher intensity. Strength training and yoga were the easiest to stick with. The authors suggest exercise could sit alongside therapy and medicines as a core treatment.

Sleep

Strong CBT for insomnia

Poor sleep and low mood feed each other. CBT for insomnia (fixed wake time, bed only for sleep, getting up if awake for long) is the recommended first treatment for chronic insomnia and often lifts mood too. Sleepio is available free on the NHS in Scotland.

Light and vitamin D

Moderate

Get daylight early in the day, especially in northern winters. Bright-light therapy can help seasonal depression. UK advice is that everyone should consider a daily vitamin D supplement (10 micrograms) in autumn and winter.

Alcohol and caffeine

Clear link

Alcohol eases anxiety for an hour or two and then worsens it, along with sleep and mood. High caffeine intake can bring on panic-like symptoms. Cutting down is one of the simplest changes to try.

People and purpose

Moderate

Isolation deepens depression. Regular contact, volunteering, groups and "social prescribing" through your GP practice (link workers who connect you to local activities) are worth asking about.

Mindfulness-based CBT

Strong for preventing relapse

An 8-week group course that combines meditation with CBT. NICE recommends it to help prevent relapse in people who have recovered from depression but are at risk of it returning.

Neuroscience and new options

Newer and specialist treatments

Most of these are for depression that has not responded to two or more standard treatments ("treatment-resistant depression", which affects about a third of people with major depression). They are given or supervised by specialists. A 2026 Mayo Clinic Depression Center expert panel reached strong consensus on three preferred next steps: adding a second-generation antipsychotic, TMS, and ketamine or esketamine.

Esketamine (Spravato) and ketamine
Specialist		Nasal spray (or IV ketamine) acting on the glutamate system, given under supervision in a clinic.FDA-approved for treatment-resistant depression since 2019, and as a stand-alone treatment since January 2025. Works within hours to days. Not routinely available on the NHS.
ECT
Established		A brief, controlled seizure under general anaesthetic, usually twice a week.The most effective option for severe, life-threatening or psychotic depression. Memory side effects are the main drawback. Underused partly because of stigma.
Psilocybin therapy (COMP360)
Not approved		A single or repeated dose of synthetic psilocybin given with psychological support in a clinic.Two phase 3 trials (about 260 and 580 patients) met their main goal in treatment-resistant depression, with rapid onset and effects lasting months. The company is seeking FDA approval. Only available in trials; do not self-medicate with mushrooms.
Digital CBT
Recommended		App or web CBT programmes with check-ins from a clinician.NICE has conditionally recommended several guided digital therapies for depression and anxiety disorders, delivered through NHS Talking Therapies in England. In Scotland, NHS boards offer online CBT programmes; ask your GP.
How do these work in the brain?

Imaging studies point to under-activity in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and over-activity in emotion circuits (amygdala, subgenual cingulate) in depression. TMS and tDCS aim to boost the prefrontal side of that network. Ketamine and psilocybin appear to trigger a burst of neuroplasticity, the growth of new connections between neurons, which may open a window in which therapy and new habits take hold more easily. Slow breathing works from the other end, through the vagus nerve, nudging the body's alarm system down.

Over the counter

Supplements: what the evidence says

Check with a pharmacist first. "Natural" does not mean safe. Some supplements interact with prescribed medicines, including antidepressants, the contraceptive pill and blood thinners. Product quality varies, so results from one brand may not apply to another.

A 2025 review that mapped every trial of over-the-counter products for depression found the most research on omega-3s, St John's wort, probiotics, saffron and vitamin D. Lavender, zinc, chamomile and others looked promising but need more data.

Prescribed treatment

Medicines: the basics

Medicines are prescribed after a conversation with a doctor about your symptoms, history, other medicines and preferences. This section is to help you have that conversation, not to choose a medicine yourself.

Give it time

Antidepressants usually take 4–8 weeks to reach full effect, and side effects often ease over the same period. It can take more than one try to find the right one.

Don't stop suddenly

Stopping abruptly can cause withdrawal: dizziness, "brain zaps", anxiety, low mood. Plan a gradual taper with your doctor and pharmacist. Most people take an antidepressant for 6–12 months, sometimes longer.

Extra checks under 25

Young adults should be reviewed closely in the first weeks of starting an antidepressant. Tell someone straight away if you feel more agitated or have thoughts of self-harm.
